\section{The \tool{} System}
\label{sec:architecture}
\label{sec:principles}

\tool{} consists of two separately installable packages (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). \texttt{tsfm\_benchmark}
generates, audits, seals and validates corpora; \texttt{tsfm\_lens} loads checkpoints through adapters, runs a gated
pipeline of analysis stages and renders the report. This section describes the parts that make cross-model
measurements valid; implementation details and usage are in Appendix~\ref{sec:usage}.

\subsection{Design principles}

Each principle below is enforced in code as a gate, a refusal or a test, so that a measurement that cannot be trusted
is withheld with a stated reason rather than reported.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,font=\normalfont\bfseries,itemsep=1pt]
\item[P1. Ground truth by construction.] Every probe is first validated on a case whose answer is known.
\item[P2. Alignment is measured, not declared.] Token spans are measured from impulse responses, and cross-model
  analysis happens only on a common time axis.
\item[P3. Causal claims need a null, a reach control and a same-seed baseline.] Every intervention is compared with a
  random intervention of matched size on the same series, and sampled models are seeded identically in compared calls.
\item[P4. The series is the unit of inference.] All intervals and $p$-values resample whole series.
\item[P5. Explore, then confirm once.] Dev results are exploratory; registered claims are tested once on a sealed
  split.
\item[P6. Every claim is typed, and failures are loud.] Each finding carries an evidence class and a generated caveat,
  and unsupported analyses are skipped with a rendered reason.
\end{description}
The evidence classes are \ev{behavioral} (input--output only), \ev{descriptive}, \ev{illustrative},
\ev{geometric} (representational similarity), \ev{linear-translatable} (one representation linearly predicts
another beyond an input baseline), \ev{causal within-model} (an intervention changes the forecast beyond a null) and
\ev{held-out confirmed} (a registered claim replicated on the sealed split). A \ev{predictive} class marks internals
that forecast a model's error without being shown to cause it.

\subsection{Controlled benchmark generation}
\label{sec:benchmark}
\label{sec:layout}

Real data enter the benchmark only as leakage references, for realism calibration and as raw material for explicitly
labeled generators. Generators come in two tiers. The \emph{synthetic} tier touches no real data and has exact ground
truth. Its \texttt{parametric} generator is additive (trend, multiple seasonalities, AR noise, changepoints,
anomalies, random walk, intermittency and heteroskedasticity), and \texttt{random\_parametric} draws a recipe from one
of eight frozen archetypes. The \emph{real-derived} tier (\texttt{mixture}, \texttt{block\_bootstrap}
\citep{kunsch1989block} and \texttt{sequential\_par}) inherits its source distribution and has only attributional
ground truth. Because ``free of leakage'' and ``realistic'' pull in opposite directions, every sample carries its tier
label and every analysis can be split by tier.

Every sample passes a two-stage leakage gate against the Monash archive \citep{godahewa2021monash}, a cheap prefilter
followed by banded DTW \citep{sakoe1978dtw}, and a cross-split near-duplicate check. Dev and private splits use disjoint
seed ranges mixed with SHA-256. Every sample is hashed, so that loading a sealed split with verification detects any
change, and fresh private \emph{epochs} are minted by striding the seed space. A validation package measures redundancy
by shape matching and diversity in the catch22 feature space \citep{lubba2019catch22}, using the participation-ratio
dimension \citep{gao2017pr} and nearest-neighbor tails rather than a 2-D embedding. It also tests dev/private
exchangeability with an energy-distance test \citep{szekely2013energy} and per-feature TOST equivalence
\citep{schuirmann1987tost}.

Two perturbation families are built in. Nine structured \emph{corruptions} (noise, detrend, deseasonalize, frequency
shift, level shift, spike, smooth, warp and dropout) drive the perturbation analysis of Section~\ref{sec:l3}.
\emph{Generator counterfactuals} move one generative parameter along a dose ladder with every random draw held fixed; dose
1.0 must reproduce the original series bit-exactly, an identity test that caught a real replay bug (\fid{BM-05}).

\subsection{Model adapters and capability tiers}
\label{sec:adapters}

A model enters through an adapter that exposes loading, a \texttt{predict} method returning point and quantile
forecasts in the original scale, the underlying module, input preparation, a forward pass and the time span of each
token. The adapter's \emph{capability tier} is derived from the methods it implements rather than declared: 0 (black
box), 1 (observable hidden states with token spans), 2 (steerable, so patching is possible) and 3 (decomposable
attention). Stages above a model's tier are skipped with the missing method named in the report. A missing capability
is a fact about the adapter; a measured failure of time localization is a fact about the model, and routes it to
behavioral analysis only.

New checkpoints can use a zero-code adapter for Hugging Face models, which discovers layers, measures token spans and
refuses rather than guesses. Its span discovery reproduces the spans of all six hand-written adapters exactly and
recovers Timer's 96-step tokens from measurement alone (\fid{MP-01}). Two of the seven models studied here use it.

\subsection{Time alignment across architectures}
\label{sec:alignment}

A token index means a different stretch of time in each model. A 480-step context is 480 tokens in Chronos-T5 and Time-MoE, 30 in
Chronos-2, Sundial and Chronos-Bolt, 15 in TimesFM and 5 in Timer, so ``token 7'' of two models covers different
inputs. Raw token positions therefore cannot be compared across models. We first measure each token's time span
$I_j$: an impulse is injected at every time step, and a token's span is the contiguous set of steps it responds to most,
accepted only if it passes a peak-to-pedestal contrast gate and a contiguity gate (Appendix~\ref{app:faq}). We then
place every model's tokens on one shared grid of windows $W_w=[wL,(w+1)L)$, with $L=32$ steps by default, by
overlap-weighted averaging:
\begin{equation}
  P_{wj} = \frac{|I_j \cap W_w|}{\sum_{j'} |I_{j'} \cap W_w|}, \qquad \tilde h_w = \sum_j P_{wj}\, h_j .
  \label{eq:pooling}
\end{equation}
Here $h_j$ is the activation vector of token $j$ in one layer, and $P_{wj}$ is the share of window $w$ that token $j$
covers. Each window's vector is a weighted average of the tokens that overlap it, in the model's own activation space.
Three cases cover all seven models (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment}):
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Tokens shorter than a window are averaged.} The 32 one-step tokens of Chronos-T5 inside a window receive
    weight $1/32$ each, and the two 16-step tokens of Chronos-2, Sundial and Chronos-Bolt receive $1/2$ each.
  \item \textbf{Tokens as long as a window map one to one.} Each 32-step TimesFM token becomes one window unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Tokens longer than a window are copied, not split.} A 96-step Timer token is the only token overlapping
    each of the three windows it spans, so all three windows receive the token's full vector with weight 1.
\end{itemize}
A token that straddles a window boundary contributes to both windows in proportion to its overlap with each. No token
is ever cut into parts, because an activation vector has no internal time axis to cut along. A window that no token
covers raises an error rather than becoming a zero row. A series-level vector is the mean of its window vectors.

\paragraph{What is compared across models.} Pooling aligns the \emph{rows} of two models' activations, not their
\emph{coordinates}. After pooling, row $(s,w)$ of every model describes the same series $s$ over the same time steps $W_w$,
but the vectors still live in each model's own space: widths differ, from 384 in Time-MoE to 1{,}280 in TimesFM, and
no axis of one model corresponds to an axis of another. No analysis therefore compares a vector of one model with a
vector of another directly. Every cross-model measure compares something defined on the shared rows:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Geometry} (CKA and RSA) compares how the rows relate to \emph{one another} within each model. Two models
    score high when the series and windows that look similar to one model also look similar to the other. This
    depends on what a window's vector keeps, which we test below.
  \item \textbf{Stitching} fits a linear map from one model's rows to the other's on training series and scores it on
    held-out series. It reports only the gain over a map from simple input features.
  \item \textbf{Concept transfer} compares which \emph{series} a feature fires on, a set of series identities that means
    the same thing in every model.
  \item \textbf{Causal effects} are measured on the forecast, which is in the time domain for every model. Interventions
    act on each model's own tokens, and patching never moves activations between models.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Is averaging fair? A test.} Averaging can discard what distinguishes the tokens inside a window, and
only models with tokens shorter than a window are averaged. We tested this directly on four disjoint sets of
v3 development series: a 128-series pilot for development, then three sets of 512 for the main run, confirmation and
validation. All criteria were fixed before each set was used (\fid{MN-38}).
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Averaging removes within-window order.} At the first captured layer, 40--71\% of token-level
    variance in the 16-step and 1-step models lies within windows. Deeper layers fall to 14--33\%, except
    Chronos-T5-Base, which stays at 70--76\% at every depth. Pooled vectors
    still decode a window's level and spread, but not its slope or shape. In the first block of Chronos-T5-Base the
    slope decodes with $R^2=0.13$ from the window mean and 0.96 from finer sub-windows. TimesFM's unpooled token keeps
    this shape.
  \item \textbf{This biases cross-model geometry.} On identical rows, averaging leaves window-level CKA unchanged
    (within 0.05, layer ranking $\rho\ge0.9$) for only 10 of 21 model pairs. The input itself shows the mechanism: its
    CKA with TimesFM's first layer is 0.15 when windows are averaged, 0.43 with four 8-step means and 0.54 with all 32
    values.
  \item \textbf{Concatenation is lossless.} The fix is to concatenate a window's tokens in time order instead of
    averaging them, $\tilde h^{\mathrm{cat}}_w=[h_j]_{I_j\subseteq W_w}$. This has no free weights. Linear CKA needs
    only the row Gram matrix, and the Gram matrix of a concatenation is the sum of the per-position Gram matrices,
    so the long vector is never formed. Concatenating 8-step means is not enough for 1-step models: Chronos-T5-Base's
    peak CKA with TimesFM keeps rising from 0.53 to 0.62 as the sub-windows shrink to single steps. A rule that adds a
    within-window trend to the mean restores decoding, but its CKA depends on how the two parts are weighted, so it
    is not used.
\end{itemize}
On a fresh set of 512 series with all captured layers, concatenation raises TimesFM's peak CKA with Chronos-2 from 0.45
to 0.73, with Chronos-Bolt from 0.39 to 0.65, with Chronos-T5-Base from 0.31 to 0.64 and with Sundial from 0.50 to
0.64, mostly at TimesFM's first layers. Pairs with Timer fall, by up to 0.10, because the other model's added order has no
counterpart in Timer's copied token. Across the 21 pairs the two rankings correlate at $\rho=0.80$. The window-level
CKA values reported from the full runs in Section~\ref{sec:rq2} use the mean and therefore understate the similarity of
TimesFM to the fine-token models. Concatenation is the tool's default (\texttt{alignment.window\_rep: concat}); the mean remains available, and the
runs reported here use it unless stated.

\paragraph{What the window rule affects.} The window rule matters only where window vectors of two models are compared
with each other: the similarity measures, namely window-level CKA, RSA and stitching. We re-measured CKA; stitching maps
the same window vectors and is subject to the same loss, but was not re-measured. The sparse-dictionary results
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}) do not depend on the rule, for three reasons.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{No cross-model vector comparison.} An SAE is fit to one model's activations, and its features are
    compared across models only through quantities defined outside activation space: \emph{which series} a feature fires
    on, and \emph{how the forecast changes} when it is removed. A series is the same series for every model, and a
    forecast is a time series in every model's output, so neither quantity depends on how a window is summarized.
  \item \textbf{Series-level codes are symmetric.} The series a feature fires on are ranked by its series-level code, the
    mean over all 15 windows. This averages the whole context for every model alike, so it carries none of the
    within-window asymmetry that biases window-level CKA.
  \item \textbf{Interventions act on tokens.} Ablations encode and decode each token separately, and the forecast is
    measured on the model's own tokens, never on windows. An SAE is admitted only if its token-level reconstruction
    preserves the forecast. This check is needed because a window-level check is unreliable for fine-token models
    (Chronos-T5-Base $\Delta$MASE $+3.8972$ at window level against $+0.2457$ at token level; \fid{MN-16}).
\end{itemize}
Pooling does enter one step of the SAE pipeline: dictionaries are trained on window vectors, so a 1-step model's
dictionary is learned from averages of 32 tokens and cannot represent within-window shape as a separate feature. This
limits what a fine-token model's dictionary can express, not how dictionaries are compared, and the token-level admission
check bounds its cost to the forecast. CONCAT cannot replace it, because a concatenated window of a 1-step model is a
$32\times768$ vector with position-specific coordinates. Training on individual tokens is the alternative; we did not
run it.

Two further costs of the shared grid are not repaired by any window rule. First, the grid is no finer than the
coarsest token. Within a 96-step span Timer has one vector, so the three windows it covers are identical, and an
alignment check can attribute an impulse to the right window at most one time in three; alignment hits are therefore
normalized by this attainable ceiling (\fid{MP-02}). Analyses of lag structure use each model's matched resolution
instead of the shared grid. Second, $L=32$ is a convention. Re-pooling the stored windows to 64 and 96 steps leaves
concept transfer and the size of geometric similarity almost unchanged, but moves the location of the peak layer pair
for 12 of 21 model pairs (\fid{SH-23}), so we report peak locations as window-dependent.

During extraction an \emph{alignment gate} checks the pooling empirically. It injects an impulse into each window and
checks, layer by layer, that the matching pooled window responds most, with the impulse amplitude calibrated per
checkpoint and context length. Hits are compared with the attainable ceiling above. Deep layers of some models lose
time localization, which the report shows and which qualifies window-level claims about those layers.

\subsection{The stage pipeline and the report}
\label{sec:stages}
\label{sec:report}

An analysis is a pipeline of 19 stages that write typed artifacts (Figure~\ref{fig:stages}). Each stage declares the
configuration keys it depends on, and these are fingerprinted, so a changed setting invalidates exactly the dependent
cached artifacts. Three gates record their decisions and the report names them as skip reasons: the \emph{tier} gate,
the \emph{run-shape} gate, which drops comparison stages when only one model is given, and the \emph{routing} gate for
models that are not time-localized. Every intervention goes through a single patching primitive, which raises an error
if the model silently splits a batch.

The deliverable is one self-contained HTML file, designed so that a reader without interpretability training can
follow it while an expert can audit every number (Figure~\ref{fig:report}). Its sections pose plain questions, such as
``Is the comparison fair?'' or ``What causally drives the forecast?''. Each stage opens with a card stating its
question, its method, what good and bad results look like and what it cannot show, and every figure carries a caption
and an expandable explanation. The summary scorecard is computed from the artifacts by printed rules, with no model
names hard-coded: each row shows a value, the reference it is compared with (a null, floor, ceiling or baseline) and the
rule that yields the verdict. Every claim is a typed record with its evidence class, a plain-language sentence and a
generated caveat. Skipped stages show the gate's reason, failed sections render visibly, and a multiplicity ledger flags
correction families that cannot reach significance.
