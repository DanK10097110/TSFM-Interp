\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Pretrained forecasting models such as TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, the Chronos family
\citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, Sundial \citep{liu2025sundial}, Timer \citep{liu2024timer}, Moirai
\citep{woo2024moirai}, MOMENT \citep{goswami2024moment}, Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2023lagllama} and Time-MoE
\citep{shi2025timemoe} are released as open checkpoints and used zero-shot. Their architectures differ sharply:
decoder-only stacks over 32-step patches, encoder--decoders over quantized single steps, encoders with cross-series
attention, decoders with sampled flow-matching heads. Leaderboards say which is more accurate. They do not say what these
models represent, whether such different architectures represent the same things, or whether any internal structure we
can name drives the forecast.

Mechanistic interpretability offers the instruments: probes \citep{alain2017probes,hewitt2019control},
representational similarity \citep{kornblith2019cka}, activation patching \citep{vig2020causal,meng2022rome,zhang2024patching},
lenses \citep{belrose2023tuned} and sparse dictionaries \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,gao2025topk}, packaged in
libraries such as TransformerLens \citep{nanda2022transformerlens} and pyvene \citep{wu2024pyvene}. Early work applies
pieces of this toolkit to single TSFMs \citep{wilinski2025exploring,mishra2026dissecting,oublal2026timesae,divo2026zeitgeist}.
Three properties of forecasting models prevent a direct transfer, and each produces silent errors rather than crashes.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \textbf{Tokens are not comparable across models.} One token covers 1 time step in Chronos-T5, 16 in Chronos-2,
    32 in TimesFM and 96 in Timer, so a cross-model comparison needs a verified mapping from tokens to time.
  \item \textbf{There is no ground truth for concepts.} Real series carry no labels for trend, period, changepoints or
    noise process, so a probe that ``finds seasonality'' cannot be checked on them.
  \item \textbf{Effects are small and easy to fake.} Removing one sparse feature typically moves a forecast by about a
    tenth of the context's standard deviation, less than the dictionary's own reconstruction error. At this scale a
    precision mismatch, an intervention the forecast head never reads, or a null measured on the wrong rows each produce
    a clean, confident and wrong result.
\end{enumerate}

We present \tool{}, a system built around these problems, and use it to ask four questions. \textbf{RQ1:} which
forecast-relevant concepts do TSFMs learn? \textbf{RQ2:} are they shared across architectures, in the sense of
responding to the same inputs, having the same effect, or both? \textbf{RQ3:} do they causally affect the forecast, and
can their importance be read from activations? \textbf{RQ4:} where in depth do these computations happen? The system is
\emph{controlled}: a synthetic benchmark with exact generative ground truth and a forecaster with planted concepts give
every instrument a case with a known answer. It is \emph{causal}: a feature counts as a concept only if removing it
changes the forecast more than removing a random direction of matched size on the same series. It is
\emph{confirmatory}: dev findings are frozen in a hash-pinned registry and tested once on a sealed private split.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \textbf{A comparative interpretability system} (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) with measured token-to-time
    alignment, a zero-code adapter path and a gated pipeline that turns a list of checkpoints into one report, together
    with a benchmark generator with leakage-audited tiers and sealed, bit-exactly regenerable splits.
  \item \textbf{A causal concept methodology} (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) that defines concepts by their effect on the
    forecast and separates \emph{same inputs} from \emph{same effect on the same inputs}.
  \item \textbf{Known-answer validation} (Section~\ref{sec:knownanswer}): every null and agreement test is held to a
    criterion fixed in advance on a forecaster with planted concepts and decoys.
  \item \textbf{A seven-model study with one-shot confirmation on two corpora} (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}
    and~\ref{sec:casestudy}), with series-level resampling, a multiplicity ledger and registered hypotheses
    (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Main findings.} We study TimesFM~2.5, Chronos-2, Sundial, Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-T5-Base, Timer and Time-MoE,
and re-test every registered claim on a second, three times larger corpus.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} Sparse-dictionary features whose removal moves the forecast more than a random direction of the
    same size exist in six of the seven models. They act mostly on level and seasonality and form a graded continuum
    rather than discrete units. Against random directions drawn from the layer's own activation distribution they
    exceed chance only 1.29-fold.
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} All 21 model pairs share geometry above chance, confirmed on a fresh sealed split, provided
    windows concatenate their tokens; averaging them, the common practice, hides 0.30--0.43 of TimesFM's CKA with the
    fine-token models. Different architectures \emph{select the same series} for a concept, held-out on both corpora and
    on raw real windows. Broad effect types recur in every model, but fine-grained concepts are organized by model: none
    spans more than three of seven.
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} Most prominent features have no held-out causal effect, and fewer than half of the registered
    single-feature causal claims replicate.
  \item \textbf{RQ4.} How TimesFM and Chronos-2 react to corruptions, layer by layer, replicates held-out for 6 of 9
    corruptions on v2 and 4 of 9 on v3 (8 of 9 keep their sign).
\end{itemize}
