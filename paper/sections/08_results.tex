\section{Results}
\label{sec:casestudy}

We first summarize what replicated on held-out data (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}) and then answer the four research
questions in turn. Each subsection opens with its answer, followed by the dev evidence and the held-out status on both
corpora. Section~\ref{sec:practical} asks whether internals help predict forecast failure, and
Table~\ref{tab:results} at the end lists every headline result with its evidence class.

\subsection{RQ1: Which forecast-relevant concepts do TSFMs learn?}
\label{sec:rq1}

\textbf{Answer.} In six of the seven models, SAE features exist whose removal moves the forecast more than removing a
random decoded direction of the same size. These features act mostly on forecast level and seasonality and form a
graded continuum rather than discrete units. Against random directions drawn from the activation distribution itself,
few single features stand out.

\paragraph{Causal features beyond isotropic directions.} Of the 56 target layers, 55 passed both reach controls. The
exception is Chronos-2's final block, where patching another layer moved the forecast by exactly 0, because its head
reads placeholder tokens that a context patch never reaches (\fid{DE-03}). Across the 55 targets, 442 feature-channel
cells clear the profile-matched null where the empirical chance rate predicts 178.75, which is 2.47 times chance. The
ratio ranges from 1.64 (Chronos-T5-Base) to 5.70 (Chronos-Bolt) for six models; for Timer it is 1.02, although its
layers do reach the forecast (relative reach 0.066--0.261; \fid{PM-17}). Tested target by target, 22 of 55 targets clear
significantly more cells than chance (Benjamini--Hochberg at 0.05), including at least two in each model except Timer,
which has none. At the level of whole features the excess is smaller: 194 features clear at least one channel, against
at most 144.95 expected if channels were independent.

\paragraph{The effects concern level and seasonality.} Nine effect families tile the causal features
(Figure~\ref{fig:families}). The two largest act on level: \emph{level raisers} (45 features) and \emph{level lowerers}
(44), which together hold 89 of the 192 assigned features. \emph{Seasonality dampeners} (25) and \emph{amplifiers} (19)
follow. Three mixed-effect families (14, 13 and 11 features), \emph{trend boosters} (11) and \emph{volatility dampeners}
(10) are smaller.

\paragraph{The effects form a continuum.} Three observations show that causal features are graded rather than
organized into discrete units. First, within a layer, 50 of 56 targets are non-modular; the 10 per-target concepts that
exist sit on 5 targets, and 4 of them are on Timer targets whose clear rate is not above chance. Second, the strict
atlas assigns only 35 of the 194 features to its 11 concepts. This is more structure than a column-shuffle null
produces (null 95th percentile of 5.05 concepts; assigned fraction 0.180 against 0.088; $p=0.005$), but it covers few
features. Third, the families cover 192 of 194 features but do \emph{not} beat the shuffle null, either in their number
($p=0.68$) or in their coverage ($p=0.23$), and the silhouette is 0.25--0.32 for every cut from 6 to 16 families. The
effect vectors of the earlier four-model run had a participation ratio of only 2.09$^{\dagger}$ (\fid{MN-07}). The
families are therefore a vocabulary for reading the effects, not discovered units.

\paragraph{Against on-manifold directions, features stand out only modestly.} The v3 run scored every feature against both
nulls. Under the isotropic null, 238 of 967 scorable features clear at least one channel. Under the covariance-matched
null, whose random directions follow the activation distribution of the same rows, 91 do. The chance count must keep
the dependence between the nine channels, because one random direction moves level and near-horizon shape together.
We therefore score each null draw as a pseudo-feature on all channels at once, leaving that draw out of its own
threshold. The expected counts are then 67.0 under the isotropic null and 70.4 under the covariance-matched null, so
features exceed chance 3.55-fold against isotropic directions but only 1.29-fold against on-manifold ones
(Poisson-binomial $p=0.0045$; Table~\ref{tab:chance}). Treating the channels as independent would put the
on-manifold expectation at 111.2, above the observed count, which overstates chance. Even so, 11 of the 24 registered single-feature claims
replicate on new series (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). The effects clearly exceed isotropic random directions but only
modestly exceed typical on-manifold directions, a distinction we return to in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{The effects do not depend on ablating to zero.} Zero ablation could move activations off the data manifold.
We therefore re-ran the 91 v3 features that clear the on-manifold null, setting each feature to its corpus mean
activation over all tokens instead of to zero, with the null matched to the removal this makes. Rerunning zero ablation
in the same setup reproduced the stored results exactly. Under mean ablation, 69 of the 91 still clear the
on-manifold null, and the effect vectors keep their direction (median cosine between the two interventions 0.997).
Survival depends on how densely a feature fires: 56 of 62 features whose mean is below a tenth of their activation on
their top series survive, but only 13 of 29 denser ones. For those dense features, mean ablation is a broader
intervention that also adds the feature on tokens where it is silent. Mean ablation hardly creates effects where zero
ablation found none: 1 of 91 sampled non-clearing features clears (\fid{MP-10}).

\paragraph{Alignment with generative factors is correlational.} After dead-feature revival, 154 of 10{,}240 TimesFM
features and 115 of 6{,}144 Chronos-T5-Base features correlate with a ground-truth generative field beyond a permutation
null; the strongest is intermittency, with $\rho=0.881$ (\fid{MP-06}). The alignment does not extend to mediation:
moving the corresponding generator parameter does not change a matched feature's forecast effect beyond chance
(\fid{MN-17}), and some vivid candidates fail seed-stability checks (\fid{PM-11}).

\subsection{RQ2: Are concepts shared across architectures?}
\label{sec:rq2}

\textbf{Answer.} Different architectures select the same series for a concept, and this replicates held-out on both
corpora and on raw real windows. The broad effect types recur in every model. At a fine grain, however, concepts are
organized by model, and agreement of causal effects on the same series is detected only between two Chronos-family
models.

\paragraph{Geometry is shared above chance but has no single meaning.} Peak CKA, the maximum over all layer pairs, is
0.342--0.426 between TimesFM and each other model, against shuffled-series nulls of 0.011--0.051. The TimesFM/Chronos-2
peak replicates on the private split (dev 0.4257; private 0.4109 [0.4014, 0.4266]) and on v3 (dev 0.444; private 0.449
[0.435, 0.464]), and the band holds across five architectures including Timer and Lag-Llama (\fid{SH-01}). Different
similarity metrics, however, rank the 21 model pairs inconsistently (Kendall's $W=0.25$, $p=0.0095$;
Figure~\ref{fig:similarity}). TimesFM/Chronos-2 is the most similar pair by error agreement (0.948) but only 15th of 21 by
CKA, where Sundial/Chronos-Bolt ranks first (0.927). The report therefore never pools similarity metrics into one score.
Two finer-grained measures fail their controls on real activations: an untrained pair scores higher than a trained,
related pair (\fid{MN-01}), and a measure of unit-level correspondence rises when both bases are rotated (\fid{SH-19}).
CKA is accordingly read only against shuffled-series nulls and never as evidence of lineage.

\paragraph{Models select the same inputs for a concept.} On dev, 1{,}296 of 1{,}584 atlas transfer tests are
reciprocal after Benjamini--Hochberg correction. Twenty transfer claims were registered from dev results alone, all
derived from two source concepts: TimesFM layer 18 (14 claims) and Sundial layer 6 (6 claims). All 20 confirm on the 971
private series, each at the smallest Holm-adjusted $p$ attainable for 20 claims at 2{,}000 null draws (0.0100), with
AUCs of 0.919--0.970 against null 95th percentiles of 0.70--0.75 (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}b). The destinations span all
seven models. Only 8 of the 20 best-matching private features are the ones selected on dev, so the confirmed claim is
that the destination layer contains \emph{some} feature selecting the same series, not that a particular feature pair
corresponds. On v3, a new set of 20 claims, from Sundial (14), Chronos-2 (5) and Time-MoE (1), confirms 20 of 20 on the
private split and 20 of 20 on the 800 raw GIFT-Eval windows (AUC 0.757--0.991). The same transfer protocol was confirmed
in three earlier one-shot looks with their own claim sets (19 of 20, then 20 of 20 twice; \fid{SH-18}). The result is
also insensitive to the alignment window: re-pooled at 64 and 96 steps, the v3 reciprocal rate moves from 0.827 to
0.801 and 0.799, and 92\% of test verdicts are unchanged (\fid{SH-23}). This is evidence about inputs, not effects.

\paragraph{The broad effect types recur in every model.} The three largest families, level raisers, level lowerers and
seasonality dampeners, each have causal members in all seven models, and volatility dampeners in six
(Figure~\ref{fig:families}b). Membership reflects each feature's own measured effect, so it does not depend on whether
the family partition beats its shuffle null. The observation is descriptive and exploratory, and Timer's members should be
read in light of its chance-level clear rate.

\paragraph{Fine-grained concepts are organized by model.} Of the 11 atlas concepts, 9 have causal members from at least
two models, but none spans more than 3 of the 7 models. The clusters are also \emph{purer} by model than random
assignment would make them: there are 9 multi-model concepts against a null mean of 10.8 ($p_{\text{below}}=0.025$), and
the mean model purity is 0.591 against 0.474 (\fid{SH-22}). By sharing class, 2 concepts are shared, 2 partially shared,
5 convergent (the same effect reached on different series) and 2 single-model (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}a); among the 9
seed-stable concepts the counts are 2, 1, 4 and 2. The structural claim holds held-out on both corpora: no concept of the
private atlas spans more than 3 of 7 models on v2 (10 concepts) or more than 2 on v3. At the grain of the atlas, each
architecture thus builds its own variants of an effect, and several arrive at the same effect independently.

\paragraph{The same effect on the same series is detected only within the Chronos family.} The shared-input test ablates
both sides of a reciprocal transfer on the same series and requires each side to clear its own null. Of 1{,}290 tests
(at most 60 per ordered model pair), 216 are scorable (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}b). Among them, 7 show the same causal
effect, 54 agree on level or shape alone, 133 show no specific agreement and 22 act differently. All 7 ``same'' verdicts
are between Chronos-2 and Chronos-Bolt, six of them through one feature set in Chronos-Bolt's fourth block. Of the 1{,}074
unscorable tests, 1{,}053 fail because one side's own ablation does not clear its null on the 21--40 shared series
(median 32). Because the test is specific but insensitive (Section~\ref{sec:knownanswer}), the 7 detections stand, while
their absence elsewhere is not evidence that effects differ.

\paragraph{Cross-model causal claims do not confirm.} None of the claims that two models share a causal effect replicates
held-out. On v2, the three registered multi-model atlas concepts fail the registered conjunctive rule. Their private
centroid cosines (0.955--0.988) exceed the null's 95th percentile (0.854--0.907; $p\le0.007$), but the rule also required
half of the member pairs to reach cosine 0.9, and with three members only one pair did; reading the centroid component
alone would be post hoc. Of the registered shared-input agreement claims, 0 of 6 tested confirm and 5 of 11 are not
testable; the best, between Chronos-Bolt block 4 and Chronos-2 block 7, reproduces its verdict ($p=0.011$) but misses
the Holm threshold (0.066). On v3 no cross-model causal claim could be registered, because all four atlas concepts sat
on targets that failed the per-target significance gate set before the data were generated ($q\ge0.243$).

\subsection{RQ3: Do concepts causally affect the forecast?}
\label{sec:rq3}

\textbf{Answer.} Single features do causally shape the forecast, and some of these effects replicate on new series at
the attainable significance floor. The effects are small, fewer than half replicate, and neither activation prominence
nor attention mass identifies them; a causal test is required.

\paragraph{Single-feature effects replicate partially.} Of the 32 single-feature causal claims registered from v2 dev,
31 were testable on the private split and 10 confirm, each with $p=0.001$ after an adaptive redraw to 1{,}000 null draws
and $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.031$, the floor for 31 tests. By model, the confirmed share is highest for TimesFM (3 of 4) and
lowest for Chronos-2 (1 of 7) and Chronos-T5-Base (0 of 4); Time-MoE, Sundial and Chronos-Bolt confirm 2 of 7, 2 of 4 and 2
of 6. By channel, near-horizon shape (4 of 8), MASE (3 of 8) and spectral centroid (2 of 4) confirm most often, and the
level and seasonal-strength claims (4 each) never do. Of the 21 claims that are not detected, 19 had not reached the
200-draw $p$ floor on dev, and one changes sign (Chronos-T5-Base block 10). On v3, under the stricter null, 11 of 24
testable claims confirm.

\paragraph{Activation prominence does not identify causal importance.} Of the 927 most prominent features of the
private atlas, 0.835 have no causal effect (one-sided lower bound 0.817; dev 0.810; $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0005$;
\fid{MN-14}); on v3 the share is 0.919 of 861. \tool{} therefore ranks features by measured effect rather than by
activation.

\paragraph{The effects are small.} On the earlier four-model run, the median single-feature effect was 0.0395 context
standard deviations$^{\dagger}$, 3.5 times smaller than the 0.139 by which substituting the SAE reconstruction moves the
forecast (\fid{MN-15}). Among the cells that clear in the v2 run, the median effect is 1.24 times the null's 95th
percentile.

\paragraph{Attention mass is not causal importance.} The attention mass that a head places on seasonal lags is
uncorrelated with the seasonal power lost when the head is ablated ($\rho=0.026$ in TimesFM and $-0.118$ in
Chronos-T5-Base; \fid{CA-04}), consistent with \citet{jain2019attention}. A minimal seasonality circuit is one head in
TimesFM but five non-additive heads in Chronos-T5-Base (\fid{CA-03}).

\subsection{What limits single-feature causal evidence}
\label{sec:limits}

The v3 run tested three ways of strengthening single-feature evidence, each judged by a criterion set in advance. None
made individual features more specific than on-manifold random directions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{More training data for the dictionaries.} Three times as many training rows raised the median
    held-out fidelity from 0.749 to 0.82. Across targets, however, fidelity is, if anything, negatively correlated with
    the share of tested features that clear the null ($\rho=-0.326$, $p=0.022$, $n=49$; \fid{MN-34}).
  \item \textbf{A more powerful per-feature test.} Using 32 series and 64 null draws per feature, with multiplicity
    control, fails the planted-concept criterion that the 8-series rule passes (Table~\ref{tab:knownanswer};
    \fid{MN-36}).
  \item \textbf{Choosing candidates by gradient attribution.} Ranking atoms by $|$activation $\times$ gradient of each
    horizon step$|$ first passed its own planted test. It nominated 620 additional features and found more causal ones
    (112 instead of 78 on 38 targets), but at no higher rate than random candidates (0.085 against 0.110, $p=0.335$),
    although it does beat random candidates against the isotropic null (0.299 against 0.192, $p=0.002$; \fid{MN-37}).
\end{itemize}
The binding limit is therefore the size of a feature's effect relative to on-manifold directions, not the amount of data,
the dictionary's quality or the choice of features to test. Since fidelity does not predict causal clarity, the
dictionary admission gate now uses fidelity only as a sanity floor of 0.5, with forecast preservation measured on 128
series; under this gate 35 of 50 v3 dictionaries are admitted instead of 8, without retraining (\fid{MN-34}).

A related question is whether the forecast change that a dictionary can express is carried by a few atoms. On 31 targets,
zeroing the five attribution-ranked atoms of a layer produces a median share of 0.74 of the combined change from the
features and the dictionary error, and on 30 of the 31 targets this exceeds activation-matched random sets of five atoms
(descriptive; \fid{D-42C}). The expressible forecast change is thus concentrated in a few atoms, but those atoms mostly do
not act more specifically than a typical on-manifold direction of the same size.

\subsection{RQ4: Where in depth, and how comparable is depth?}
\label{sec:rq4}

\textbf{Answer.} The way TimesFM and Chronos-2 react to corruptions across depth largely replicates held-out, with one
corruption unstable across corpora. Absolute depth locations are comparable only after accounting for captured compute
and the depth convention.

\paragraph{Depth-resolved reaction profiles replicate.} For TimesFM and Chronos-2, the rank correlation between their
per-layer corruption fingerprints replicates on the v2 private split for 6 of 9 corruptions (overall $\rho=0.524$ [0.504,
0.544]; Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}a). The two models react at matching depths to detrending (private $\rho=0.368$) and
dropout (0.510), at opposite depths to noise ($-0.743$), spikes ($-0.785$) and warps ($-0.651$), and not systematically
to deseasonalizing (0.086 [$-0.027$, 0.186]). Under the registered rule, which requires the dev value to fall inside the
private interval, three corruptions do not replicate. Two of them show a \emph{stronger} effect on private data:
frequency shift (0.381 against 0.216 on dev) and smoothing ($-0.581$ against $-0.418$). The third, level shift, changes
sign (dev $-0.188$; private 0.109 [$-0.025$, 0.159]). On v3, 4 of 9 corruptions replicate under the strict rule and 8 of 9
keep their dev sign. Level shift flips sign again (dev $-0.164$; private 0.252), so it is the one depth agreement that is
not stable across corpora.

\paragraph{Where restoration happens.} In TimesFM, restoration by per-window patching concentrates in the window just
before the horizon and grows with depth, from 0.068 at layer 0 to 0.412 at layer 16 for the noise corruption on the
earlier four-model run$^{\dagger}$ (exploratory, $n\approx300$; \fid{DE-01}).

\paragraph{Depth locations need two qualifications.} Captured compute ranges from 13.9\% (Chronos-T5-Base) to 99.8\%
(Timer) of a forecast's FLOPs (\fid{DE-05}), and Chronos-T5's last encoder block sits at depth 0.478 on the whole-stack
axis (\fid{DE-04}); the report renders both next to every depth claim. Moreover, no layer chosen by one of three layer
selectors was robust to the choice of selector for any model (\fid{DE-08}).

\subsection{Can internals predict forecast failure?}
\label{sec:practical}

If internal features carry causal information, they might tell a user when to distrust a forecast. For each model we
predict a series' log MASE (scored by Spearman correlation) and a failure flag (scored by AUROC) from free,
output-only features: quantile width, flatness and catch22 features of the context. We then add the SAE-family
activations at the last window and measure the gain, cross-fitted with a series bootstrap on 941 dev series
($n_{\text{boot}}=1000$). The evidence class is \ev{predictive}: a feature that predicts error is not shown to cause it.

The free baseline is strong (Spearman 0.76--0.85). For five of the seven models (Chronos-2, TimesFM, Sundial,
Chronos-T5-Base and Chronos-Bolt), the gain's interval includes zero for both tasks (log-MASE Spearman gains $-0.004$ to
$+0.005$, AUROC gains $-0.010$ to $+0.008$). The gain is positive for the two weakest models, Timer (+0.030 [0.019,
0.043]; baseline 0.787; failure rate 0.30) and Time-MoE (+0.012 [0.005, 0.019]; baseline 0.853; failure rate 0.62),
robustly across five fold seeds; no failure-AUROC gain excludes zero. Both gains were registered and confirm when the
frozen procedure is refit on the private split ($n=935$): Timer 0.0446 [0.0292, 0.0605], $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.002$, and
Time-MoE 0.011 [0.0032, 0.0194], $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.012$. Fourteen dev comparisons were run and two were selected, which
is legitimate because the private split referees the selection. On v3 both confirm again, while a newly registered
Chronos-T5-Base claim does not.

Internals do not help choose a model. Routing each series to the model with the lowest predicted MASE gives a mean MASE of
1.948 [1.814, 2.070] with the baseline features and 1.964 [1.837, 2.093] with internals added (difference $-0.016$
[$-0.044$, 0.010], $p=0.258$), and both are worse than always using Chronos-2 (1.856; $p=0.001$; oracle 1.523). Internals
add information exactly where the free baseline is weakest.
