\opening{Dear Editors,}

We submit our manuscript \emph{TSFM-Lens: Controlled, Causal and Confirmatory Interpretability for Time-Series
Foundation Models} for consideration as a regular paper in the Journal of Machine Learning Research.

Time-series foundation models are now used zero-shot for forecasting, but the interpretability methods developed for
language models do not carry over directly. Tokens span different lengths of time in different architectures, real
series carry no labels that could validate a probe, and the causal effects of internal features are small enough that
uncontrolled measurements mislead. The manuscript presents an open-source system built
around these three problems and the results of applying it to seven models.

The main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A comparative interpretability system.} A measured token-to-time alignment puts architectures with
  tokens of 1 to 96 time steps on a common axis, and a leakage-audited synthetic benchmark with exact generative ground
  truth and sealed development and private splits supplies the data. A 19-stage pipeline produces one report in which
  every claim carries its evidence class.
  \item \textbf{A causal concept methodology.} A sparse-autoencoder feature counts as causal only if removing it changes
  the forecast more than removing the same per-token amount along a matched random direction on the same series, after
  reach controls confirm that the intervention lands. Concepts are then compared across models in two separate senses:
  whether models respond to the same inputs, and whether the effects on the forecast agree.
  \item \textbf{Known-answer validation.} Each null and test is first run on a forecaster with planted concepts and
  decoys, against a pass criterion fixed in advance. The profile-matched null passes (false-positive rate at most
  0.003, sensitivity 0.70--1.00); a uniform null, a more powerful per-feature test and a subspace redesign fail and are
  reported as such.
  \item \textbf{A seven-model study confirmed once on two corpora} (TimesFM, Chronos-2, Sundial, Chronos-Bolt,
  Chronos-T5, Timer and Time-MoE). Claims were registered on development data and tested once on sealed private data:
  43 of 82 replicable claims confirm on the first corpus and 41 of 63 on a second, three times larger corpus.
  Different architectures select the same input series for a concept: all 20 registered transfer claims confirm on
  both private splits and on 800 raw real-world windows. Their causal machinery, however, is organized by model, and no
  concept spans more than three of the seven models. Activation prominence is a poor guide to causal importance:
  83.5\% of prominent features have no held-out effect. Single causal features survive mean ablation (69 of 91) but
  exceed random directions drawn from the activation distribution only 1.29-fold, and neither more data, a more
  powerful test nor gradient-selected candidates changes this. Depth-resolved reaction profiles replicate for most
  corruptions, with one that changes sign across corpora.
\end{enumerate}

We report negative results with the same weight as positive ones, since several of the instruments we first used
would have produced confident false positives.

We believe the paper suits JMLR because its contribution is methodological as much as empirical: it shows that
interpretability claims about forecasting models need known answers, matched nulls and held-out
confirmation, and it supplies a tool that makes these the default. All code, the benchmark generators, a complete
seven-model example run with its held-out confirmation, and a reproduction command for every reported number are
public under the MIT license at \url{https://github.com/DanK10097110/TSFM-Interp}, archived at
\href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23267462}{doi:10.5281/zenodo.23267462} (version 1.1.0) and installable from PyPI.
