\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Pretrained forecasting models such as TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, the Chronos family
\citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, Sundial \citep{liu2025sundial}, Timer \citep{liu2024timer} and Time-MoE
\citep{shi2025timemoe} are released as open checkpoints and used zero-shot. They are built very differently. TimesFM
reads 32-step patches with a decoder; Chronos-T5 quantizes every time step into a vocabulary and runs an
encoder--decoder; Chronos-2 is an encoder with cross-series attention; Sundial samples its forecast from a flow-matching
head; Timer reads 96-step tokens. Leaderboards rank them by accuracy, but say nothing about what they represent. In
vision and language, networks trained on similar data are argued to converge on similar representations
\citep{li2016convergent,huh2024platonic}. Whether forecasting models with such different inductive biases do the same,
and whether shared structure, where it exists, drives the forecast, is open.

We answer this question for seven TSFMs. Doing so requires solving three problems that the interpretability toolkit of
language models \citep{kornblith2019cka,meng2022rome,bricken2023monosemanticity} does not address, each of which produces
confident wrong answers rather than errors. Tokens cover between 1 and 96 time steps depending on the model, so
activations must first be placed on a common time axis. Real series carry no labels for trend, seasonality or noise, so a
probe that ``finds seasonality'' cannot be checked on them. And removing one internal feature moves a forecast by about a
tenth of the context's standard deviation, a scale at which a poorly chosen null or a precision mismatch produces
spurious effects. We measure each model's token-to-time map, generate benchmarks with exact generative ground truth,
validate every causal instrument on a forecaster with planted concepts and decoys, and test every registered claim once
on a sealed held-out split, on two independent corpora (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{The models agree on what in a series matters} (Section~\ref{sec:shared}). All 21 model pairs share
    representational geometry above chance, confirmed on a fresh sealed split. Concepts transfer across architectures
    at the level of inputs: for a concept found in one model, features in other models select the same series, and all
    registered transfer claims confirm on both corpora and on 800 raw real-world windows held out from all development.
  \item \textbf{They implement the response differently} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}). Causal features act mainly on
    forecast level and seasonality, and level raisers, level lowerers and seasonality dampeners exist in all seven
    models. But fine-grained concepts are organized by model, and none spans more than three of seven; the same
    capability can be one attention head in one model and five interacting heads in another; and forecast-relevant
    information is distributed, so single features stand out only modestly against directions drawn from the
    activation distribution. The same causal effect on the same series is detected only between two Chronos-family
    models.
  \item \textbf{Shortcuts imported from language-model interpretability mislead on TSFMs}
    (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}). Averaging the tokens in a time window, the usual way to compare models with different
    tokenizers, hides 0.30--0.43 of TimesFM's similarity to fine-token models. Most of the strongest-firing dictionary
    features (83.5\%) have no held-out causal effect. Attention mass on seasonal lags is uncorrelated with which heads
    carry seasonality. A random-direction null that ignores a feature's per-token profile inflates the count of causal
    features 2.7-fold.
\end{enumerate}
We also find that the depth at which TimesFM and Chronos-2 react to input corruptions replicates held-out for most
corruptions, and that internals improve forecast-failure prediction only for the two least accurate models
(Section~\ref{sec:depth}). Every result carries an \emph{evidence class} (behavioral, geometric, causal within-model,
predictive, held-out confirmed) and an entry in a public findings ledger, \repo{FINDINGS.md}, with exact numbers and a
reproduction command; identifiers such as \fid{SH-18} link to it. The analysis system, \tool{}, and the benchmark
generator are open source and described in Appendix~\ref{app:system}.
