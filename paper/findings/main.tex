\begin{abstract}
Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) are built in very different ways, and whether they learn similar representations is unknown.
We compare seven TSFMs on a common time axis measured from their tokens, using representational similarity, sparse dictionaries and causal ablation.
Each causal test is validated on a forecaster with planted concepts, and pre-registered claims are tested once on held-out data from two independent benchmarks.
The models agree on which series matter.
When a feature in one model responds selectively to a set of series, every other model has a feature that responds to the same set, and in every model the causal features act mainly on the forecast's level and seasonality.
Their representations are more similar than chance even after removing the input, though the strongest similarity lies in early layers that mostly encode it.
The models rarely agree on how a feature changes the forecast: matching effects appear only within one model family, and single features are barely more influential than random directions in the model's activations.
All held-out claims about shared inputs held, and none about shared effects did.
We also show that averaging the tokens in a time window, a common alignment step, changes which models appear similar.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
time-series foundation models, mechanistic interpretability, representational similarity, sparse autoencoders, causal intervention
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Forecasting models pretrained on large collections of time series, such as TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, the Chronos family \citep{ansari2024chronos,ansari2025chronos2}, Sundial \citep{liu2025sundial}, Timer \citep{liu2024timer} and Time-MoE \citep{shi2025timemoe}, are now used zero-shot in place of models fitted to each dataset.
They are built in very different ways.
TimesFM is a decoder that reads 32-step patches; Chronos-T5 turns every time step into a token and uses an encoder--decoder; Chronos-2 is an encoder; Sundial samples its forecast from a flow-matching head; Timer reads 96-step tokens.
Benchmarks rank these models by accuracy but do not tell us what they compute.
For vision and language models it has been argued that networks trained on similar data converge to similar representations \citep{li2016convergent,huh2024platonic}.
We ask whether the same is true of TSFMs, and whether what they share affects the forecast.

Three practical problems stand in the way.
Tokens of different models cover different stretches of time, from one step to 96, so activations cannot be compared position by position.
Real series carry no labels for properties such as trend or seasonality, so a feature that seems to track seasonality cannot be checked.
And removing one internal feature typically changes a forecast by about a tenth of the context's standard deviation, which is small enough for a poorly chosen baseline to produce spurious effects.
We therefore measure the time span of each model's tokens and compare all models on a common grid of 32-step windows, use generated benchmarks whose components are known, validate every causal test on a synthetic forecaster with planted concepts, and test pre-registered claims once on held-out data from two independently generated corpora.

The answer depends on what is compared.
The models agree on inputs.
If a feature in one model responds selectively to a set of series, every other model has a feature that picks out the same set; all 40 registered claims of this kind held on held-out data, and the 20 from the second corpus held again on raw real-world series.
The features that affect the forecast also have similar broad effects in every model, most of them raising or lowering its level or strengthening or weakening its seasonality.
The models rarely agree on effects.
Features with the same effect on the same series were found only between Chronos-2 and Chronos-Bolt, two models of the same family, and no registered claim of a shared effect held.
The effects are also weak and diffuse: a dictionary feature changes the forecast only a little more than a random direction drawn from the model's own activations, and effects vary continuously instead of falling into groups.
Representational geometry lies in between.
Every pair of models is more similar than chance, also after removing what the input predicts, but the strongest similarity is in the first layers and partly reflects a common encoding of the input.

Two further results concern method.
Claims of different kinds replicated at very different rates: those about shared inputs all held, about a third to a half of those about single features held, and none about shared effects did.
And two habits carried over from language models gave misleading answers: averaging the tokens in a window, which hides much of the similarity between models with different token widths, and ranking dictionary features by activation, which mostly selects features without effect.
Code, benchmark generators and a reproduction command for every number in the paper are public.\footnote{\url{\repourl}. Appendix~\ref{app:ledger} maps each result to its entry in the repository's results ledger.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Time-series foundation models and their interpretation.}
Pretrained forecasters differ mainly in how they turn a series into tokens and how they produce a forecast \citep{liang2024foundation}.
Patches of consecutive steps, introduced by PatchTST \citep{nie2023patchtst}, are used with different lengths by TimesFM, Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai}, MOMENT \citep{goswami2024moment} and Timer; Chronos quantizes single steps and trains a T5 model \citep{raffel2020t5}, Chronos-Bolt and Chronos-2 predict quantiles of many steps at once, Time-MoE routes single steps through a mixture of experts, and Sundial samples from a flow-matching head \citep{lipman2023flow}.
Most of them normalize each series internally \citep{kim2022revin}, and they are compared by accuracy on collections such as GIFT-Eval \citep{aksu2024gifteval}.
Explanations of time-series models have mostly attributed predictions to input time steps \citep{tonekaboni2020fit,crabbe2021dynamask,ismail2020benchmarking}, which describes behavior rather than internal representations.
Mechanistic work on TSFMs is recent and studies one model at a time: \citet{wilinski2025exploring} steer trend and periodicity by adding directions to the residual stream, \citet{mishra2026dissecting} relate sparse-autoencoder features of Chronos to causal effects, \citet{oublal2026timesae} and \citet{divo2026zeitgeist} search for interpretable concepts, and \citet{yildirim2026superposition} examine superposition in forecasting transformers.
Our results bear on how such studies are read, because single features in our models are only slightly more influential than random directions in the model's activations.

\paragraph{Interpretability methods and their pitfalls.}
We use standard tools: activation patching \citep{vig2020causal,meng2022rome} and sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with TopK activations \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2024sae,gao2025topk}, whose features vary across training seeds \citep{paulo2025seeds} and are often matched by simple baselines \citep{kantamneni2025sparse}.
Crosscoders \citep{lindsey2024crosscoders} learn one dictionary for several models; we instead compare independently trained dictionaries through the series their features select and the effects they have, which needs no shared coordinates.
Interpretability methods can produce convincing results that do not reflect the model: saliency maps can ignore the model they explain \citep{adebayo2018sanity}, attention weights are not explanations \citep{jain2019attention}, a direction's apparent meaning can depend on the data used to inspect it \citep{bolukbasi2021illusion}, and subspace interventions can suggest mechanisms the model does not use \citep{makelov2024illusion}.
We guard against these failures by validating each test on planted concepts, and against selective reporting by pre-registration \citep{nosek2018preregistration} and a single use of held-out data \citep{dwork2015reusable,gelman2014garden}.

\paragraph{Comparing representations.}
CKA \citep{kornblith2019cka}, SVCCA \citep{raghu2017svcca}, representational similarity analysis \citep{kriegeskorte2008rsa} and model stitching \citep{bansal2021stitching} have been used to argue that networks converge \citep{li2016convergent,huh2024platonic}.
These measures can disagree with one another and with functional differences between models \citep{ding2021grounding,klabunde2025similarity}, and CKA can be moved substantially by transformations that leave behavior unchanged \citep{davari2023cka}.
We therefore read every similarity value against a null, report stitching as a gain over an input baseline and never pool different measures.
Previous comparisons also take for granted that the compared representations refer to the same inputs.
For models that tokenize time differently this correspondence has to be constructed, and we show that the usual construction changes the conclusions.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
\label{sec:benchmarks}
Because real series carry no ground truth for the properties we study, all analyses use generated corpora.
Synthetic series are sums of known components (trend, seasonalities, autoregressive noise, changepoints, anomalies, random walks, intermittency, heteroskedasticity) and use no real data.
Real-derived series are made from real data by mixing, block bootstrapping \citep{kunsch1989block} or sequential synthesis; they are more realistic but have no exact ground truth.
Every series passes a leakage check against the Monash archive \citep{godahewa2021monash}.
The primary corpus has 965 development series (410 real-derived) and a held-out split of 971.
The independently generated replication corpus has 2{,}876 development series (785 real-derived, from 15 sources) and a held-out split of 1{,}024; a second held-out split of it was used for the geometry claims.
In addition, 800 raw windows from 12 GIFT-Eval datasets serve as a real-world test set.
Contexts have 480 steps and the horizon is 64 steps.

\paragraph{Exploration and confirmation.}
\label{sec:confirmation}
Everything computed on the development splits is exploratory.
Selected results were written down as hypotheses, and the registry was hashed before the corresponding held-out split was generated; each hypothesis was then tested once.
Of 128 registered hypotheses for the primary corpus and 111 for the replication corpus, 82 and 63 could be tested.
Confidence intervals and $p$-values resample whole series, never windows \citep{efron1993bootstrap}, and each type of claim is one Holm family \citep{holm1979}.
A few results come from an earlier two-model study made with the same software; we mark them where they appear.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{A Common Time Axis}
\label{sec:alignment}

A 480-step context becomes 480 tokens in Chronos-T5 and Time-MoE, 30 in Chronos-2, Sundial and Chronos-Bolt, 15 in TimesFM and 5 in Timer.
We find the time span of each token by injecting an impulse at every time step and recording which tokens respond; a span is accepted only if the response is strong and contiguous.
For every model with a hand-written adapter, this recovers the documented spans exactly.
We then cut the context into windows of $L=32$ steps and represent each window by the concatenation, in time order, of the tokens inside it (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment}):
\begin{equation}
\tilde h_w = \big[\,h_j\,\big]_{I_j\subseteq W_w}, \qquad W_w=[wL,(w+1)L),
\label{eq:pooling}
\end{equation}
where $I_j$ is the span of token $j$.
A TimesFM token fills exactly one window; a Timer token spans three windows and is assigned to each.
Concatenation keeps the order of time steps inside a window, which averaging the tokens would discard.
After alignment, row $r$ of every model's activation matrix refers to the same series and window, although the columns of different models are unrelated.
All comparisons go through these shared rows; activations are never moved from one model to another.

\subsection{Representational Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry-method}

Centered kernel alignment (CKA) compares two sets of representations of the same inputs by how similarly they arrange those inputs.
For centered window representations $A$ and $B$ it is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CKA}(A,B)=\frac{\|B^{\top}A\|_F^2}{\|A^{\top}A\|_F\,\|B^{\top}B\|_F},
\end{equation}
which is 1 when one representation is a rotation and rescaling of the other and does not require the two to have the same width \citep{kornblith2019cka}.
CKA depends on the data only through the Gram matrix $AA^{\top}$, and the Gram matrix of a concatenation is the sum of the Gram matrices of its parts, so the concatenated vectors of Equation~\eqref{eq:pooling} never need to be formed.
We compute CKA for every pair of layers of every pair of models and compare it with a null obtained by permuting whole series (1{,}000 permutations).
We also fit ridge maps between layers \citep{bansal2021stitching} and report how much variance they explain beyond a map from input features alone.

\subsection{Dictionary Features and Their Causal Effects}
\label{sec:causal}

For each analyzed layer we train a TopK sparse autoencoder \citep{gao2025topk},
\begin{equation}
z(h)=\operatorname{TopK}\big(W_e(h-b_d)+b_e\big), \qquad \hat h=W_d\,z(h)+b_d ,
\end{equation}
which writes each activation $h$ as a sparse combination of learned directions, the features.
To measure what feature $i$ does, we take the 8 series on which it is most active and run the model twice with the layer replaced by the reconstruction $\hat h$, once with $z_i$ set to zero:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_i^{(c)}(x) = g_c\big(f(x;\,h\leftarrow\hat h_{-i})\big) - g_c\big(f(x;\,h\leftarrow\hat h)\big).
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
Here $f$ is the forecasting model and $g_c$ measures one of nine properties of the forecast: trend, seasonal strength, spectral centroid, level, dispersion, near- and far-horizon shape, MASE and flatness.
Both runs use the reconstruction, so the dictionary's reconstruction error cancels.
Before any feature is scored we check that patching a layer with its own activations leaves the forecast unchanged and that patching it with another layer's activations does not, and models with sampled heads are run with the same random seed in every compared call.

Whether an effect is large has to be judged against a baseline.
We remove the same amount of activation, at every token, along 16 random directions on the same series, and call a feature causal for property $c$ if its mean absolute effect exceeds the 95th percentile of these random effects.
In the primary corpus the random directions are drawn uniformly in the dictionary's code space (the isotropic null).
In the replication corpus they are drawn from a Gaussian with the covariance of the layer's activations (the activation-matched null), so that they point in directions the model actually uses.
Because a mean over several series is compared with a percentile of single-series effects, a random direction is called causal more or less often than 5\% of the time; we measure this chance rate by scoring each random direction against the others.

\subsection{Concepts and Two Kinds of Sharing}
\label{sec:sharing}

By a concept we mean a causal feature together with the set of series on which it is most active.
Two models can share a concept in two senses.

They share inputs if some feature of the second model separates the concept's series from the remaining series.
We measure this by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the best such feature, which is 0.5 for a feature unrelated to the concept and 1 for a perfect separation.
Because the best of thousands of features will separate almost any set of series to some degree, the null repeats the same search for random sets of series matched in generator family.
A transfer is reciprocal if it holds in both directions; the false discovery rate is controlled within each ordered model pair \citep{benjamini1995fdr}.

They share an effect if, on the same series, the two features change the forecast in the same way.
For each reciprocal transfer we ablate both features on the shared series, after checking that each is causal there.
The effects agree if both their concordance in forecast level and their cosine similarity in forecast shape exceed values obtained by ablating random sets of features with matched activation.
They act differently only if both fall below the 5th percentile of those values, since failing to exceed the 95th percentile is not by itself evidence of a difference.

To compare many features at once, we describe each causal feature by its effects on the nine properties, in units of the null's 95th percentile.
The atlas clusters these effect vectors from all models (complete linkage at cosine similarity 0.9), so that an atlas concept is a set of features with closely matching effects.
A coarser clustering into effect families (average linkage at 0.5) summarizes the kinds of effect.

\subsection{Validation on Forecasters With Planted Concepts}
\label{sec:knownanswer}

A poorly chosen null does not raise an error; it produces wrong results that look like any others.
We therefore run every test first on a synthetic forecaster whose concepts are known.
One block of the forecaster writes fixed directions into its residual stream on series with a given property, and its output head turns them into a known change of level, trend, seasonality or dispersion.
It also contains decoys: directions that respond to inputs but have no effect, effects too weak to detect, and a layer with no planted effect.
For the cross-model tests, two such forecasters of different width, depth and basis share some planted concepts.
Pass criteria were fixed in advance (Table~\ref{tab:knownanswer}).
Both nulls used in the study pass.
A plausible alternative, which removes a single batch-averaged amount of activation instead of the amount the feature removes at each token, fails on every forecaster.
The same-effect test never declared two decoys to share an effect but found a truly shared concept in only two of five forecasters, so its positive results can be trusted and its negative results say little.

\subsection{What the Models Share}
\label{sec:shared}

\paragraph{Geometry.}
\label{sec:geometry}
Every one of the 21 model pairs has layers whose CKA exceeds the permutation null, on both corpora.
On the replication corpus the best layer pair of each model pair was registered and tested on a new held-out split, where all 21 again exceeded their nulls; for TimesFM and Chronos-2 the held-out CKA is 0.707 (95\% interval 0.679 to 0.734) against 0.712 in development.
Ridge maps between the best layer pairs explain on average 0.55 more variance than a map from input features.

The obvious objection is that two models may look alike simply because both encode the same input.
For most pairs the most similar layers are indeed the first ones, which closely track the input (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}b).
The three models with 16-step tokens reach CKA of 0.93 to 0.94 with one another in their first layers, and each of those layers has CKA between 0.84 and 0.91 with the input window itself.
To remove the input, we regressed every layer on the 32 values of its window, cross-fitted over series, and recomputed CKA on the residuals (development split of the replication corpus, 512 series).
All 21 pairs stay above a permutation null of the residuals, but the median CKA of the best layer pair falls from 0.62 to 0.40 (Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}a).
Pairs that involve Chronos-T5-Base or the first layer of TimesFM keep 37\% to 63\% of their similarity, while seven pairs, all involving Timer or Time-MoE, keep at least 96\%.
Since the regression removes only what is linear in the window's own values, these residual values are an upper bound on similarity beyond the input.
The models do arrange series similarly beyond what the input dictates, in line with the convergence reported for other domains \citep{huh2024platonic}, but the largest CKA values partly measure a shared encoding of the input.

The choice of window representation matters as much as the input.
If a window is represented by the average of its tokens, the usual choice when token widths differ, the held-out CKA of TimesFM with Chronos-2, Chronos-T5-Base, Chronos-Bolt and Sundial falls by 0.30 to 0.43; this reduction was confirmed for all four pairs on the second held-out split.
Models with short tokens encode the order of steps within a window across several tokens, and averaging erases that order.
In the first block of Chronos-T5-Base, for instance, the slope of a window can be read from the window average with $R^2=0.13$ but from finer sub-windows with $R^2=0.96$.
Averaging also reorders the pairs: TimesFM and Chronos-2, the most similar pair by agreement of their forecast errors, rank 15th of 21 by CKA with averaged windows and 9th (primary) or 7th (replication) with concatenated ones.
Pairs involving Timer barely change, since a 96-step token has no internal order to lose.

\paragraph{Inputs.}
\label{sec:inputs}
Geometry compares whole layers; concept transfer asks whether individual features respond to the same series.
On the development split of the primary corpus, 1{,}296 of 1{,}584 transfer tests are reciprocal.
We registered 20 transfer claims from two concepts, one in layer 19 of TimesFM's 20 layers and one in layer 7 of Sundial's 12, with destination features in all seven models.
All 20 held on the held-out split, with AUCs of 0.92 to 0.97 against null 95th percentiles of 0.70 to 0.75 (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}b).
On the replication corpus, 20 new claims from concepts in Sundial, Chronos-2 and Time-MoE held both on the held-out split and on the 800 raw real-world windows, with AUCs of 0.76 to 0.99.
The claims concern the existence of a matching feature rather than its identity: only 8 of the 20 best held-out matches are the features chosen in development.
The fraction of reciprocal transfers barely depends on the window length (0.83 at 32 steps, 0.80 at 64 or 96).
Because the source concepts sit deep in their models, this agreement is not limited to input-encoding layers.
It shows that all seven models draw the same distinctions between series; it does not show that those distinctions are more than simple statistics of the input would give.

Figure~\ref{fig:exemplar}a--c gives an example.
Features of Chronos-2, Chronos-Bolt and Sundial with closely matching effects share 6 to 12 of their 20 most active series, where 0.41 would be expected by chance.
Removing the Chronos-2 or the Chronos-Bolt feature on the same intermittent series raises the forecast level, by 0.02 and 0.07 standard deviations of the context.

\paragraph{Kinds of effect.}
\label{sec:families}
The causal features of all seven models fall into a few kinds of effect (Figure~\ref{fig:families}).
Nine effect families cover 192 of the 194 causal features of the primary corpus.
The two largest raise (45 features) or lower (44) the level of the forecast, and the next two dampen (25) or amplify (19) its seasonality.
Features that raise the level, lower it or dampen seasonality occur in every model.
So the models not only attend to the same series but also push their forecasts in the same few directions, mostly level and seasonality.

\subsection{What the Models Do Not Share}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

\paragraph{Concepts are organized by model.}
\label{sec:bymodel}
The atlas contains 11 concepts, more than shuffled effect vectors produce ($p=0.005$), but none contains features from more than three models.
Features group by model more than chance predicts: 9 concepts have members from two or more models, against 10.8 expected under the null ($p=0.025$).
In five concepts, different models produce the same effect on different series, as in Figure~\ref{fig:exemplar}d,e, where features of Chronos-Bolt and TimesFM that both raise the forecast level share none of their top series.
On held-out data, no concept contains more than three models on the primary corpus or more than two on the replication corpus.

\paragraph{Same effect on the same series.}
\label{sec:sameeffect}
We applied the same-effect test to the 1{,}290 reciprocal transfers of the primary corpus.
Only 216 could be scored; in the rest, at least one of the two features had no detectable effect on the shared series.
Of the 216, 7 show the same effect, 54 agree in level or in shape only, 133 show no specific agreement and 22 act differently.
All 7 agreements are between Chronos-2 and Chronos-Bolt.
Since the test made no false agreement on the planted forecasters, we take these 7 as real.
Since it is also insensitive, the absence of agreement elsewhere does not by itself show that effects differ, although the 22 cases show that they sometimes do.
None of the registered claims of a shared effect held.

\paragraph{Single features are weak and not specific.}
\label{sec:specificity}
Against the isotropic null, 442 combinations of feature and forecast property in the primary corpus are causal, where 178.75 would be expected by chance; the excess ranges from 1.64-fold in Chronos-T5-Base to 5.70-fold in Chronos-Bolt.
Timer is the exception: its layers influence the forecast, but its features are causal no more often than chance.
On the replication corpus, where every feature was scored against both nulls, the excess is 3.55-fold against isotropic directions but only 1.29-fold against activation-matched directions.
In other words, removing a typical direction from the model's own activations changes the forecast nearly as much as removing a dictionary feature.
The effects are also small: the median feature moves the forecast by 0.097 standard deviations of the context, less than half the change caused by replacing the layer with its reconstruction.
Of the single-feature claims, 10 of 31 held on the primary corpus and 11 of 24 on the replication corpus.

The effects that do exceed the stricter null are largely robust to how the feature is removed.
We re-tested the 91 such features with three other interventions (setting the feature to its mean, to its value on another series, or subtracting it from the original activations rather than the reconstruction); 79 stay causal under at least one and 37 under all three, and the direction of the effect is preserved under the first two (median cosine 0.997 and 0.991).
Which features pass, however, depends on the intervention.

Nor do the effects form discrete groups.
In 50 of 56 layers the effect vectors cannot be clustered into groups of at least three features, and there are no more effect families than in shuffled data ($p=0.68$).
This is not because the effects are all alike: the effect vectors have a participation ratio of 5.58 out of 9.
With the weak specificity of single features, this suggests that what matters for the forecast is spread over many directions and varies continuously, unlike the discrete units sought in language models \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity}.

\subsection{Practices That Mislead}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

Besides averaging tokens (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}), three practices common in the interpretability of language models gave misleading answers here.

\paragraph{Ranking features by activation.}
Most SAE analyses start from the features that are most often or most strongly active.
In TSFMs these are mostly without effect: of the 927 most prominent features in the held-out data of the primary corpus, 83.5\% have no causal effect (one-sided 95\% lower bound 81.7\%), and on the replication corpus 91.9\%.
Ranking by estimated effect works much better.
Zeroing the five features ranked highest by a gradient-based attribution produces a median of 74\% of the change in the forecast that the dictionary can express, more than random sets of five activation-matched features in 30 of 31 layers (development data of the replication corpus).

\paragraph{Choosing the null.}
Whether a feature counts as causal depends on what it is compared with.
In a four-model development run, the batch-averaged null that failed validation raised the number of causal features from 110 to 293, and even between the two valid nulls the excess over chance differs by almost a factor of three.
Measures of model similarity disagree in a similar way: seven of them, including geometry, agreement of errors and concept transfer, rank the 21 model pairs differently (Kendall's $W=0.25$), which is why we report each against its own null and do not combine them.

\paragraph{Attention weights and generative factors.}
In the earlier two-model study, the attention a head paid to seasonal lags was unrelated to the seasonality lost when the head was removed (Spearman correlation 0.026 in TimesFM and $-0.118$ in Chronos-T5-Base), as \citet{jain2019attention} found for language models.
Dictionary features also correlated with generative factors of the synthetic series, most strongly with intermittency (Spearman correlation 0.881), but in a registered test on 13 layers, varying a factor in a controlled way changed the matched feature no more than activation-matched random features (none of 151 tests significant after correction).

\subsection{Depth and Failure Prediction}
\label{sec:depth}

For each of nine input corruptions, we measured how much the residual stream changes at each layer and compared TimesFM and Chronos-2 by the rank correlation of these depth profiles.
The comparison held for 6 of 9 corruptions on the primary corpus (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}a; overall Spearman correlation 0.524) and 4 of 9 on the replication corpus, where 8 of 9 correlations kept the sign of their development estimate.
The models react at similar depths to detrending and dropout and at opposite depths to noise, spikes and warping.
Depth comparisons need care, because the captured layers account for between 13.9\% (Chronos-T5-Base, whose decoder is not captured) and 99.8\% (Timer) of a forecast's computation; we therefore compare profiles rather than the positions of single layers.

Internal features say little about when a model will fail.
Predicting a series' log MASE from what is visible without internals (interval width, forecast flatness and catch22 features of the context; \citealp{lubba2019catch22}) already gives Spearman correlations of 0.76 to 0.85, and adding dictionary features helps significantly only for the two least accurate models, Timer and Time-MoE, where the small gain held on both held-out splits.
Routing each series to the model with the lowest predicted error is worse than always using Chronos-2, with or without internal features.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why inputs agree and effects do not.}
A simple account fits most of what we see.
Which properties of a series matter for its forecast, such as its level, the strength of its seasonality or whether it is intermittent, is a fact about the data, and any competent forecaster has to detect them.
How a model turns that detection into a forecast depends on choices that differ between models: how the series is normalized, how many steps a token covers, and whether the output comes from a quantile head, a patch decoder or a sampler.
On this account the models should agree on which series a feature selects and on the rough direction of its effect, and differ in the details of the effect.
That is the pattern we find, and the one pair with matching effects, Chronos-2 and Chronos-Bolt, shares both training lineage and output design.
It is a weaker form of convergence than the one proposed for vision and language models \citep{li2016convergent,huh2024platonic}.

\paragraph{How strong is the case for different mechanisms?}
The evidence for shared inputs is direct; the evidence for different mechanisms is weaker.
For it: the atlas groups features by model more than chance would, 22 of the 216 scorable same-effect tests show different effects, and in the earlier two-model study the same seasonal function was carried by one attention head in TimesFM and by five interacting heads in Chronos-T5-Base.
Against it: the same-effect test found a truly shared concept in only two of five planted forecasters, and 1{,}074 of 1{,}290 tests could not be scored.
There is also a structural reason for caution.
If forecast-relevant information is spread over many directions, removing one feature removes an arbitrary part of a larger mechanism, and two models computing the same function could still disagree feature by feature.
The defensible conclusion is that we find no shared mechanism across model families at the resolution of single features, and some evidence against one.
Interventions on learned subspaces \citep{geiger2021causal}, dictionaries trained jointly on several models \citep{lindsey2024crosscoders} or stronger interventions could test the question at a coarser resolution and might change the answer.

\paragraph{Implications for practice.}
Studies of single TSFMs \citep{mishra2026dissecting,oublal2026timesae,divo2026zeitgeist} should compare a feature's effect with random directions drawn from the model's own activations: against isotropic directions most features look important, against activation-matched ones few do.
The importance of a feature should be established by intervention, because activation strength, attention weights and correlation with a generative factor all pointed to the wrong components here.
Comparisons across models need a measured time axis and a window representation that keeps the order of time steps.
Finally, between a third and a half of our registered claims failed on held-out data, and the failures were concentrated in the claims that would have been most interesting, about shared effects, single features and depth.
We do not think a study of this kind can be done reliably without registration and a single held-out test \citep{gelman2014garden}.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\label{sec:limitations}
We study seven transformers whose tokens cover contiguous time intervals; models that read non-contiguous lags, such as Lag-Llama \citep{rasul2023lagllama}, are not included.
With seven models, differences between them are confounded with architecture, size and training data, and Timer's features, which do not exceed the null, carry no information about causal concepts.
The analysis is univariate, so Chronos-2's attention across series is not studied, and the decoder of Chronos-T5 and the flow-matching head of Sundial are not captured.
Exact ground truth exists only for synthetic series, and overlap with the models' pretraining data cannot be excluded.
Raw real-world series are poorly suited to causal analysis because weakly predictable series often receive nearly constant forecasts \citep{wan2026collapse}, which leaves an ablation nothing to change; real data therefore enter through real-derived series and the external test set.
The confirmed transfers come from a small number of concepts (two in the primary corpus, from three models in the replication corpus), and results about concepts depend on the size, sparsity and seed of the dictionaries, which our replicate seeds measure but cannot remove.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Time-series foundation models built in very different ways pick out the same series and push their forecasts in broadly the same directions, but we could not find a mechanism that two model families share.
Whether this reflects genuinely different solutions or the limited resolution of single-feature interventions is the main question the study leaves open.
The benchmarks, the analysis software and a reproduction command for every number are public, so the comparison can be extended to new models and to coarser interventions.

\paragraph{Layers and dictionaries.}
The layers to analyze are chosen per model by a screening step that finds where the residual stream changes most and where its trajectory across layers bends; it selects 56 layers in the primary corpus (TimesFM 10, Chronos-2 9, Sundial 9, Time-MoE 9, Chronos-T5-Base 9, Timer 6, Chronos-Bolt 4) and 50 in the replication corpus.
Each dictionary keeps the $k=32$ largest activations per token.
Its size is the largest from 192 to 6{,}144 at which at most 30\% of features are inactive, and inactive features are revived with an auxiliary loss \citep{gao2025topk}.
Each dictionary is trained for at least 720 steps with three seeds; 9 of the 11 atlas concepts of the primary corpus are found with all three.
The median held-out fraction of variance explained ranges from 0.647 (TimesFM) to 0.878 (Chronos-T5-Base), and reconstruction fidelity does not predict how clearly a layer's features act on the forecast (Spearman correlation $-0.326$ across 49 layers).
Retraining all 50 dictionaries of the replication corpus from the recorded configuration reproduced them exactly, including the same 91 causal features.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Bootstrap intervals use 5{,}000 resamples of series.
For per-series differences $d_s$ with resampled means $\bar d^*_b$, $b=1,\dots,B$,
\begin{equation}
p=\min\Big(1,\ \max\big(\tfrac1B,\ 2\min\{\tfrac1B\#(\bar d^*_b\le 0),\ \tfrac1B\#(\bar d^*_b\ge 0)\}\big)\Big),
\end{equation}
floored at $1/B$ \citep{phipson2010permutation}, so a Holm family of $m$ tests cannot reach a $p$-value below $m/B$; this bound is checked for every family.
In the transfer test the statistic is the largest AUC over all destination features, and the null recomputes the same maximum for random sets of series, so the search is part of the null; a registered transfer claim uses the larger $p$-value of its two directions.
Before a held-out result is read, the minimum detectable effect of each claim is estimated by simulation at power 0.8; for the accuracy claim it is a MASE difference of 0.133, against an observed 0.209.
Single-feature claims have low power because each feature is tested on 8 series; 19 of the 21 unconfirmed ones had not reached the smallest attainable $p$-value in development.
For the input control of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, each window is z-scored by the mean and standard deviation of its 480-step context, every token position of a layer is regressed on the window's 32 values by ridge regression (5 folds split by series, $\lambda=10^{-3}n_{\mathrm{train}}$), and the null permutes whole series of one model's residuals 200 times.

\paragraph{Integrity of the held-out tests.}
Each registry was frozen and hashed before its held-out split was generated, and each held-out test was run once.
The held-out split of the primary corpus shares no series with seven earlier splits.
Registered claims were selected from larger pools of development results (for example 20 of 1{,}124 transfer candidates and 32 of 113 single-feature candidates); the held-out data decide each claim, so the selection does not bias the test.

\paragraph{Compute and software.}
The full analysis of the primary corpus took 21.1 hours on one NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU.
The software is written in Python with PyTorch and records the environment of every run.
It consists of one package that generates, audits and seals benchmarks and one that loads models through adapters, runs the analyses and writes a report; two of the seven models were loaded with a generic adapter for Hugging Face checkpoints that measures token spans instead of assuming them.
